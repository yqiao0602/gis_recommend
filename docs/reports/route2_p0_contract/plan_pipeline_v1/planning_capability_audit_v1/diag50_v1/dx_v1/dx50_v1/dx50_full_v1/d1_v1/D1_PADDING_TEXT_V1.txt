Paper is made from plant fibres that have been separated and suspended in water. The earliest sheets were formed by dipping a mould into a vat and lifting a thin layer of pulp, which was then pressed and dried. Over the centuries the tools changed, but the underlying method remained much the same: fibres are broken down, spread evenly, drained, pressed, and left to dry in the air.

The fibres most often used came from flax, hemp, cotton, and later from wood. Each material behaves a little differently. Flax gives long, strong fibres that interlock well. Cotton produces a softer sheet with a smoother surface. Wood fibres are shorter and require more careful preparation, but they can be supplied in large quantities. The choice of material affects the colour, the weight, and the way the finished sheet takes ink.

Sizing is the treatment that reduces the tendency of a sheet to absorb liquid too quickly. Starch, gelatine, and similar substances were brushed onto the surface or mixed into the pulp. A well-sized sheet holds a sharp edge and does not feather when written upon. Unsized paper is more absorbent and is often used for purposes where quick drying matters more than precision.

Colour and texture were adjusted in different ways. Pulp could be bleached in the sun or with chemical agents. Dyes were added to the vat. Texture came from the mould, from the felt used during pressing, and from the surface treatment applied afterwards. A rough surface catches pigment; a smooth surface reflects light more evenly.

Binding brought separate sheets together. The leaves were folded, gathered into sections, and sewn to cords or tapes. The sewing supported the spine while allowing the pages to open. Covers were made from boards, leather, cloth, or paper, and the choice depended on how often the volume was expected to be handled. A heavy book needed a stronger structure; a small pamphlet could be held together with a few stitches.

Adhesives played a part as well. Animal glues were strong but stiff; pastes made from starch were more flexible but slower to dry. Bookbinders learned to combine them, using one where strength was needed and another where movement had to be allowed. Repairs followed the same logic: a sound repair respects the way the parts move against one another.

Storage and handling shaped many of the choices above. Damp air softens glue and encourages mould. Dry air makes leather brittle and paper fragile. Light fades dyes and darkens certain papers. Shelves that hold volumes upright keep the pages under gentle pressure; boxes protect delicate covers from dust and abrasion. Careful handling, more than any single treatment, extends the life of a bound volume.

Ink was prepared by mixing a colouring substance with a binder and a liquid. Soot, lampblack, and iron salts were common colouring substances; gum arabic and oil served as binders. The proportions determined how the ink flowed, how quickly it dried, and whether it sat on the surface or sank into the fibres. A thin ink suits a smooth sheet; a thicker ink suits a porous one.

Type and handwriting placed different demands on the surface. A press transfers ink under pressure, so the sheet must be strong enough to resist the squeeze. A pen relies on capillary action, so the surface must draw the liquid evenly. Papermakers adjusted the furnish and the finish to suit the intended use, and binders selected materials with those adjustments in mind.

Wear appears first at the joints. The hinge of a cover flexes every time the volume is opened, and the sewing supports carry the weight of the sections. Reinforcement at these points is invisible when it is done well. A spine lining that is too stiff will crack; one that is too soft will sag. The best result comes from matching the flexibility of the lining to the movement of the text block.

Repair work calls for restraint. A treatment that is reversible can be undone if a better method is found later. Materials that age in the same way as the original are preferred to materials that are stronger but incompatible. The aim is not to make an old volume look new, but to slow further change while keeping the evidence of its history.
